\begin{abstract}
A model could reconstruct every patch of a photograph and still be unable to put the photograph back together. We study this distinction with Regenerative Autoencoders (ReGAE), a family of models that reconstruct a sample by repeated denoising. After a reconstruction is damaged, the same denoiser can be applied again to repair it, a process we call \emph{regeneration}. Our example is a 237-megapixel James Webb Space Telescope image of Pandora's Cluster, a cluster of galaxies. A first model learns to reconstruct individual patches; a second learns the large-scale structure of the mosaic. In this hierarchy, the first model's responses to the image become the data for the second.
\end{abstract}

\section{Conclusion}
ReGAE uses a denoiser to reconstruct a sample and to resume that reconstruction after damage. In the Pandora mosaic, the same architecture serves a second purpose. The decoder uses the code of a patch to reconstruct its pixels. The gencoder uses a neighborhood of codes to predict what the encoder would produce for the next view. This turns one aspect of the first model's behavior into a learning problem in its own right. The distinction matters when a generated view is plausible but the trajectory appears to lose its place: the models can preserve what a region looks like while leaving its location unresolved. That remaining ambiguity gives a concrete question for a further level of modeling.
