\begin{abstract}
A model could reconstruct every patch of a photograph and still be unable to put the photograph back together. We use this distinction to ask what it takes to learn a dataset through reconstruction. Regenerative Autoencoders (ReGAE) are a family of models that reconstruct a sample by repeated denoising. If the reconstruction is damaged, the same denoiser can be reapplied to repair it; we call this \emph{regeneration}. We apply ReGAE to a 237-megapixel James Webb Space Telescope image of Pandora's Cluster, a cluster of galaxies. One model learns to reconstruct small patches; a second learns how their representations change as the view shifts across the image. In exploratory runs, we observed plausible star fields when sampling patches alone. With the second model, the sampled views remained coherent over larger regions, although they sometimes appeared to jump between locations. The example illustrates how one model's representation can become the data for another.
\end{abstract}

\section{Conclusion}
Return to the shuffled photograph. A decoder can reproduce each patch from its code, yet recovering the photograph also requires knowing where the patches belong. Saving their order supplies that knowledge; training on their neighborhoods asks a model to acquire some of it. ReGAE lets us pose both problems with the same denoising architecture. As a decoder it reconstructs pixels from a patch code and can resume after damage. As a Gencoder it predicts a neighboring view in the space of patch codes. The reported jumps between apparently plausible views expose the remaining distinction: predicting what could come next is a weaker requirement than recovering what was there. Asking what information a new level captures, and what still has to be supplied, gives recursive meta-modeling a concrete research question.
