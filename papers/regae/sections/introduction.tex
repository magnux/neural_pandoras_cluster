\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Why ask a model to reconstruct its training data? Consider two images with the same class label. A classifier can answer correctly for both while discarding the differences between them. A model asked to reconstruct each image must preserve, or be given, enough information to tell them apart at the chosen fidelity. For studying what it means to learn a dataset in its own right, we favor reconstruction for precisely this reason: it gives us a way to ask which distinctions the model has kept.

What, then, must a model learn to reconstruct a dataset? Suppose we cut a photograph into patches. A model can learn to give each patch a compact description, called a \emph{code}, and to reconstruct the patch from that description. Now rearrange the codes like the tiles of a picture puzzle. The same patches can still be reconstructed, but placing them in their new order scrambles the photograph. Even knowing how frequently each code occurs leaves this problem unsolved: the photograph contains information in the arrangement of its patches as well as in their appearance. If we supply the original order, we supply some of the very information we want to explain.

\emph{Regenerative Autoencoders} (ReGAE) are a family of models built around a denoiser that can act repeatedly on the same state. In an autoencoder, an encoder network produces the code and a denoiser serves as the \emph{decoder}: given that code, it updates a provisional reconstruction. It can start with noise, or it can resume from a reconstruction that has been damaged. Neural cellular automata use the term \emph{regeneration} for this recovery after damage, as distinct from growing a pattern in the first place~\cite{nca}. Neural Cellular Automata Manifold (NCAM) used an encoder to select a cellular growth program for reconstruction~\cite{ncam}. ReGAE retains the idea that reconstruction is a process one can restart and gives the job of updating the state to a denoising network.

The choice of a denoiser also lets us turn the question posed by the shuffled photograph into a learning problem. We study a James Webb Space Telescope mosaic of Pandora's Cluster~\cite{webb}. The mosaic spans nearly 237 megapixels. A small crop may resolve a star or part of a galaxy yet reveal little about where the crop belongs. First we train a ReGAE to reconstruct patches. We then keep its encoder fixed and train a second denoiser on neighborhoods of the resulting codes, asking it to predict what a window will contain after a small shift. We call this denoiser the \emph{Gencoder} because its role is to generate codes. The decoder and Gencoder use the same kind of network, with separate weights; their names describe the jobs they perform.

The wider question is how much of a dataset a system can come to understand by modeling first the data and then its own learned representations. We call the second step \emph{meta-modeling}. Spatial order is the relevant relationship in this mosaic. In a collection of labeled images, by contrast, the first model might encode whole images and a second might learn how those codes vary within and across classes. We study Pandora's Cluster to make the distinction between sample content and relationships observable, rather than to optimize a benchmark score. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the family, and Sec.~\ref{sec:evidence} follows the two models from patches to extended views. We then compare ReGAE with other denoising and generative models (Sec.~\ref{sec:related}) and discuss what this example can tell us about recursive meta-modeling (Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion}).
