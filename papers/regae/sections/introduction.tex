\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

If one aim of intelligence is to extract and organize all the information in a dataset, how could a learning system go about it? We approach the question through reconstruction. A classifier can give two images the same correct label while discarding what distinguishes them. A system asked to reconstruct each image must preserve those differences to the extent that the reconstruction is meant to be faithful. This gives us something concrete to inspect: what can the system recover, and what must we still tell it?

Consider the sliding-tile galaxy beside the abstract. Suppose a model learns a compact description, or \emph{code}, from which it can reconstruct each tile. Slide a tile into the empty square. Its code can still reproduce every star on that tile, yet a spiral arm that used to meet at an edge now breaks there. Even a list of every tile and its frequency would be identical before and after the move. To reconstruct the photograph we also need their arrangement. This is one reason compression is a useful lens on learning: a regularity we can predict from context is a detail we need not specify in full each time. There is a second question even for one tile: how much must its code say about this particular patch, and how much does the decoder already know about drawing galaxies?

\emph{Regenerative Autoencoders} (ReGAE) are a family of models built around a denoiser that can act repeatedly on the same state. In an autoencoder, the encoder produces a code and the denoiser serves as the \emph{decoder}: given that code, it updates a provisional reconstruction. It can start with noise or resume from a reconstruction that has been damaged. Neural cellular automata use the term \emph{regeneration} for recovery after damage, as distinct from growing a pattern in the first place~\cite{nca}. Neural Cellular Automata Manifold (NCAM) used an encoder to select a cellular growth program for reconstruction~\cite{ncam}. ReGAE gives the task of repeatedly updating the state to a denoising network.

We study the two aspects of the tile puzzle in a James Webb Space Telescope mosaic of Pandora's Cluster~\cite{webb}. At nearly 237 megapixels, the mosaic contains structures much larger than any patch used to train the autoencoder. The first model learns to encode each patch and reconstruct its pixels. We then hold the encoder fixed and ask a second denoiser to predict the codes it will produce for a neighboring view. This denoiser serves as the \emph{gencoder}. Its targets record how the encoder responds to the image, at the bottleneck through which the decoder receives its description of each patch. We could have observed another layer; we chose the bottleneck because its codes must support reconstruction in a compact form.

We call this second step \emph{meta-modeling}: one model learns the behavior of another over a dataset, as observed at a chosen layer. Here that behavior is spatial because the patches come from a single mosaic. For a collection of labeled images, a first model might encode whole images and a second might learn how its responses vary within and across classes. Pandora's Cluster lets us inspect one such composition without making the two spatial levels a requirement of the ReGAE family. We describe the family and the case study first (Secs.~\ref{sec:method}--\ref{sec:evidence}), then compare the update with other generative models and consider what further levels might capture (Secs.~\ref{sec:related}--\ref{sec:discussion}).
